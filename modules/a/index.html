Module A — Visual & Sensory Assessment

Read before you test

What your eyes, nose, and tongue can tell you about water — and, just as important, what they can't. These are reference signals, never a test result: many dangerous contaminants change nothing about how water looks, smells, or tastes.

Visual contamination reference guide

What discolored, cloudy, or sediment-laden water usually indicates (offline photo library, not a diagnostic claim).

Yellow or orange water

Usually iron. The EPA's secondary (non-health-based) guideline for iron is 0.3 mg/L; above that, water commonly shows a rusty color, visible sediment, a metallic taste, and reddish-orange staining on fixtures.

  • EPA secondary standard for iron: 0.3 mg/L
Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Black or brown water

Usually manganese. The EPA's secondary guideline is 0.05 mg/L; above that, water can show black-to-brown color, black staining, and a bitter metallic taste.

  • EPA secondary standard for manganese: 0.05 mg/L
Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Blue or blue-green water

Usually copper, often from corroding copper pipes. The EPA's secondary guideline is 1.0 mg/L; above that, water can show a metallic taste and blue-green staining on fixtures, sinks, or laundry.

  • EPA secondary standard for copper: 1.0 mg/L
Sources: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals; CDC / ATSDR — Copper — ToxFAQs

Cloudy, milky, or hazy water

Often just trapped air bubbles clearing naturally within a few minutes, especially after cold weather or nearby pipe work. It can also reflect elevated total dissolved solids (TDS); the EPA's secondary guideline for TDS is 500 mg/L, above which water can show hardness, deposits, coloring, and a salty taste.

  • EPA secondary standard for total dissolved solids: 500 mg/L
Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Green, blue-green, red, or brown water — possible algal bloom

A bloom of algae or cyanobacteria ("blue-green algae") can discolor a water supply and may look like scum, mats, or paint on the surface of standing water. Some blooms produce toxins. If local officials report cyanobacteria or toxins in your tap water, follow their guidance; if you notice this kind of discoloration yourself, contact your water utility or health department and switch to bottled or otherwise treated water for drinking and cooking until it's resolved — boiling does not remove these toxins and can concentrate them.

Source: CDC — How to Recognize a Harmful Algal Bloom

None of this tells you the water is unsafe — or safe

These are secondary, aesthetic guidelines, not health-based safety limits, and discoloration or cloudiness does not by itself mean water is dangerous. But it also doesn't rule danger out. Treat this as a prompt to test, not a verdict either way.

Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Odor reference guide

Sulfur, chemical, and musty/algae smells, and what they commonly indicate.

Rotten-egg smell

Usually hydrogen sulfide, often from naturally occurring sulfur bacteria in a well or plumbing system. It's a nuisance odor, not on its own a marker of danger, but it's worth a well test if it's new or worsening.

Source: CDC — Well Water Safety

Musty or earthy smell

Often decaying organic matter or algae in the source water or a storage tank, more common with seasonal changes.

Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Strong chemical smell

Could be normal chlorine disinfection at a treated municipal supply, or could indicate something has entered the water that shouldn't be there (fuel, solvent, pesticide). A strong, unfamiliar chemical odor — especially from a well or after a nearby spill or flood — deserves prompt testing, not routine dismissal.

Source: CDC — Well Water Safety

Odor threshold reference

The EPA's secondary guideline for odor is 3 threshold odor number (TON), commonly associated with rotten-egg, musty, or chemical smells.

  • EPA secondary standard for odor: 3 TON
Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Taste reference guide

Metallic, salty, bitter — informational only.

Metallic taste

Most often copper or iron. The EPA's secondary guideline for copper is 1.0 mg/L; higher household exposure can also cause stomach upset, so persistent metallic taste plus new corrosion signs (blue-green staining, corroding pipes) is worth testing.

Sources: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals; CDC / ATSDR — Copper — ToxFAQs

Salty taste

Usually chloride or sulfate; the EPA's secondary guideline for each is 250 mg/L. Can also reflect elevated total dissolved solids (500 mg/L guideline) or, near coastlines, saltwater intrusion into a well.

  • EPA secondary standard for chloride and sulfate: 250 mg/L each
Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Bitter taste

Often manganese; the EPA's secondary guideline is 0.05 mg/L, above which water can taste bitter and metallic.

Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Chlorine or chloramine taste

A normal, expected taste or smell from a municipal supply that disinfects with chlorine or chloramine — it's a sign the utility is treating the water, not a defect. It's regulated by the EPA and, at the levels utilities use, doesn't make people sick.

Source: CDC — About Water Disinfection with Chlorine and Chloramine

Taste alone doesn't confirm safety

Water that tastes fine can still carry contaminants that have no taste at all. Don't let "it tastes normal" substitute for testing, especially on a private well.

Source: CDC — Well Water Safety

Iron/manganese/rust staining explainer

Common tap and fixture staining causes, not necessarily a health risk.

Reddish-orange staining

Iron. EPA secondary guideline: 0.3 mg/L. Shows up on sinks, tubs, and laundry as rust-colored staining.

  • Iron: 0.3 mg/L
Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Black staining

Manganese. EPA secondary guideline: 0.05 mg/L.

  • Manganese: 0.05 mg/L
Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

Blue-green staining

Copper, typically from corroding copper pipework. EPA secondary guideline: 1.0 mg/L.

  • Copper: 1.0 mg/L
Sources: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals; CDC / ATSDR — Copper — ToxFAQs

These are aesthetic (secondary) standards, not health limits

The EPA sets these as non-enforceable guidelines for taste, odor, and appearance, separate from its health-based primary standards. Staining is a nuisance-and-diagnostic signal about your plumbing or source water, not by itself a safety verdict.

Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

"When visual checks aren't enough" explainer

Reinforces that appearance can't rule out pathogens; always err toward treat/boil.

Most dangerous contaminants are invisible

Many of the contaminants that matter most for health — including disease-causing germs and chemicals like arsenic — typically have no color, taste, or odor. Water that looks, smells, and tastes completely normal can still be unsafe.

Sources: CDC — Well Water Safety; CDC — Guidelines for Testing Well Water

Lead is a clear example

You cannot see, taste, or smell lead dissolved in water. It most often comes from lead pipes, solder, or fixtures, and testing is the only reliable way to know whether it's present — waiting for a warning sign that will never come is not a safety plan.

Source: EPA — Basic Information about Lead in Drinking Water

The reverse is also true

Water that looks or smells unpleasant (for example, from sulfur or minerals) isn't automatically unsafe to drink — it may just be a nuisance issue. Appearance, smell, and taste are not a reliable stand-in for either direction.

Source: EPA — Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals

What to do instead

If you're on a private well or rainwater system, test at least once a year for bacteria and common local contaminants, more often if anything changes. If you can't test and you're unsure, treat or boil before drinking — every time, regardless of how the water looks.

Source: CDC — Guidelines for Testing Well Water
