Region-specific water-risk reference

Offline database of known regional issues — arsenic, fluoride, lead pipes, cholera zones. Informational only.

Contamination sources vary by place, not just by source type

What's likely to be in your water depends heavily on local geology, local infrastructure age, and local land use — naturally occurring arsenic in some groundwater regions, agricultural runoff (nitrate) near farmland, aging lead service lines in older cities, and more. There's no single universal risk profile.

Source: CDC — What Causes Tap Water Contamination

Use this as a starting prompt, not a verdict

Knowing your region's common issues tells you what to test for and ask your utility about — it doesn't replace an actual test result for your specific water.

Source: CDC — What Causes Tap Water Contamination

A few known regional patterns in the U.S.

The northeastern U.S. has areas of naturally high arsenic in bedrock that can leach into well water during drilling. Nitrate contamination tracks closely with nearby farmland, from fertilizer and other agricultural chemicals reaching groundwater. Lead risk tracks with infrastructure age, not geography — older cities with lead service lines and lead-soldered joints carry the highest risk regardless of region.

Source: CDC — What Causes Tap Water Contamination

Boil-water-advisory checklist

Day-to-day changes: brushing teeth, ice, pet water, baby formula.

Drinking, cooking, and brushing teeth

Use bottled water, or bring water to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 feet / about 1,000 meters elevation) before drinking it, cooking with it, or brushing your teeth with it. See the Purification Methods module's boiling guide for why PlainWater uses this more cautious altitude threshold instead of the higher one some other sources cite.

Source: CDC — Boil Water Advisory

Ice and refrigerator dispensers

Don't use ice or water from a refrigerator or freezer dispenser, or from any appliance connected to your water line, until the advisory is lifted.

Source: CDC — Boil Water Advisory

Pets

Give pets bottled water, or boiled water that has cooled — the same standard as for people.

Source: CDC — Boil Water Advisory

Infant formula

If you formula-feed, use ready-to-feed formula if you can get it, to avoid mixing formula with water at all. If you must mix formula with tap water during an advisory, boil the water first and let it cool.

Source: CDC — Boil Water Advisory

Dishes and hand-washing

Disposable plates/cups/utensils are the simplest option during an advisory. For hand-washing, tap water and soap are generally fine — the main risk during a boil-water advisory is swallowing the water, not touching it.

Sources: CDC — Boil Water Advisory; CDC — Guidelines for Personal Hygiene During an Emergency

"Is my tap water safe" decision tree

Municipal vs. well vs. disaster context, branching to the right purification method.

On a public (municipal) supply, normally

Your utility is required to test regularly and publish an annual water quality report. Under a boil-water advisory or after a known contamination event, follow the specific guidance issued for that event instead of routine assumptions.

Source: CDC — About Drinking Water

On a private well or rainwater system

No one else is testing it for you. Routine annual testing is on you, and appearance/taste/smell being normal doesn't confirm safety.

Source: CDC — About Drinking Water

In a disaster or after an advisory of any kind

Default to treat-or-boil regardless of source, until you have specific confirmation the water is safe again.

Source: CDC — Boil Water Advisory

Lead/old-pipe safety guide

Risk signs, tap-flushing guidance, special care for infant formula prep.

Why lead matters more for infants

Formula-fed infants can get a large share of their total lead exposure from the water used to mix formula, because of how much they drink relative to their body size.

Source: EPA — Actions to Reduce Potential Lead Exposure

Flush the tap before using water that's been sitting

Water that has sat in pipes for hours picks up more lead. Run the cold tap, take a shower, or do a load of laundry or dishes before drawing water for drinking or cooking — ask your utility how long to run it in your area, since it depends on your service line.

Source: EPA — Actions to Reduce Potential Lead Exposure

Always use cold water for drinking, cooking, and formula

Hot water dissolves lead faster than cold water. Never draw water for drinking, cooking, or infant formula from the hot tap — and note that boiling does not remove lead.

Source: EPA — Why can't I use hot water from the tap for drinking, cooking, or making baby formula?

The biggest risk factor is your service line and fixtures

Homes built before the mid-1980s, or connected by a lead service line, carry the highest risk. If you don't know your service line material, your water utility can often tell you.

Source: CDC — About Lead in Drinking Water

Wildfire-ash contamination guidance

Post-fire water safety.

Why fires can compromise water quality

During and after a wildfire, ash and combustion byproducts settle on streams, lakes, and reservoirs, and fire can damage the treatment and distribution infrastructure itself. Source-water quality issues can persist for years after a large fire.

Source: CDC — Private Wells after a Wildfire

If you're on a private well after a wildfire

Test for bacterial contamination at minimum; if the well components show damage, testing for volatile and semi-volatile organic compounds by a certified lab is recommended.

Source: CDC — Private Wells after a Wildfire

On a public supply

Check directly with your drinking water provider before assuming normal service has resumed.

Source: CDC — Private Wells after a Wildfire

Handling ash around the home

Avoid washing ash into storm drains, and wash any fruit or vegetables from a garden that ash has fallen on before eating them.

Source: EPA — Protect Yourself from Ash

Flood-specific water safety guidance

What changes after floodwater exposure.

Default to bottled water after a flood

It's safest to drink bottled water until you're certain your water is free of contamination. If extensive flooding occurred or you suspect the well may be contaminated, don't drink it.

Source: CDC — Safety Guidelines: Floodwater

Shallow and older wells are at highest risk

Dug wells, bored wells, and other wells shallower than about 50 feet are more likely to be contaminated after flooding, even if there's no visible damage.

Source: CDC — Safety Guidelines: Floodwater

Chemical or fuel smell is a hard stop

If your water smells like fuel or chemicals, or you're in an area at risk for a fuel/pesticide release, contact your local health department — boiling or standard disinfection will not make chemically contaminated water safe.

Source: CDC — Safety Guidelines: Floodwater

Well disinfection after a flood is a real, involved process

It generally needs professional help from a well/pump contractor or your local health department, not just a home fix.

Source: CDC — How to Disinfect Wells After an Emergency

Earthquake-specific water storage/safety guidance

Storage and sourcing after seismic events.

Store before it happens

Keep at least one gallon of water per person per day for several days, stored in the original sealed container, in a cool dark place, unopened until needed — earthquakes can cut off both power and piped water with no warning.

Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Water

After a quake, check before you trust the tap

Damaged pipes, cracked mains, and compromised treatment plants are common after a significant earthquake. Treat tap water as suspect until your utility confirms it's safe, and rely on your stored water or another verified safe source in the meantime.

Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Earthquakes

Shut off your main water valve if you suspect damage

Cracked lines can let contamination into your house's water, so it's a good idea to shut off your water at the main valve until authorities confirm it's safe. Learn where your main shut-off valve is and how to use it before an earthquake happens, not after — every household member should know its location.

Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Earthquakes

The water already in your pipes and water heater is a source

Shutting off the main valve promptly traps the water already sitting in your house's pipes and water-heater tank, which you can then use as an emergency source rather than losing it to drainage or backflow.

Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Earthquakes

Never turn gas back on yourself

If you shut off gas service for any reason after a quake, leave it off until a qualified professional turns it back on — this isn't a water-safety step directly, but it's part of the same "check utilities before you trust them again" mindset.

Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Earthquakes

Hurricane evacuation water checklist

Pre- and post-evacuation water steps.

Before you evacuate or shelter in place

Fill clean containers and any bathtubs/sinks you can, in addition to your stored emergency water — one gallon per person per day is the baseline planning figure.

Sources: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Water; FEMA — How to Prepare for a Hurricane

After the storm passes

Don't assume municipal water is safe just because the storm has passed — hurricanes routinely cause both flooding contamination and infrastructure damage. Wait for an explicit all-clear from your utility, and treat/boil in the meantime if you must use tap water.

Source: FEMA — How to Prepare for a Hurricane

Chemical/industrial spill response guidance

Industrial and agricultural runoff response.

If you suspect chemical contamination

Don't drink the water. Contact your drinking water utility or local health department right away for guidance specific to the contaminant involved.

Source: CDC — Chemicals That Can Contaminate Tap Water

Boiling will not help here

Unlike biological contamination, boiling does not remove chemical contaminants — in some cases it can concentrate them by evaporating off the water. Rely on bottled water or another verified safe source instead.

Sources: CDC — Chemicals That Can Contaminate Tap Water; CDC — Guidelines for Treating Well Water

On a well, near known industrial or agricultural activity

Wells near industrial sites or farmland carry an elevated ongoing risk for chemical contamination (solvents, fuel, pesticides, nitrate) — that's a reason for more frequent, targeted testing, not just after an obvious spill.

Source: CDC — Chemicals That Can Contaminate Tap Water

If you got sick

Report it to your local health department — individual reports help identify contamination events faster.

Source: CDC — Chemicals That Can Contaminate Tap Water
