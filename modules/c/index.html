Every way to make it safe

Every way to make water safe to drink, matched to what's actually in it — from a rolling boil to a DIY sand filter. Boiling and chemical disinfection kill or inactivate germs; filtration physically removes them; nothing here removes every hazard by itself, so when in doubt, combine methods.

Step-by-step boiling guide

Altitude-adjusted boil times.

Boiling is the most reliable method

Boiling kills or inactivates essentially all disease-causing germs, including bacteria, viruses, and parasites like Giardia and Cryptosporidium — more reliably than any chemical or filter alone.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

How long to boil

Bring water to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute. At altitudes above 5,000 feet (about 1,000 meters), boil for at least 3 minutes instead — water boils at a lower temperature at altitude, so the extra time matters.

  • 1 minute at normal altitude; 3 minutes above 5,000 ft / 1,000 m
Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

After boiling

Let the water cool naturally, then store it in a clean, sanitized, covered container. Boiling doesn't remove dissolved chemicals, heavy metals, or improve taste/appearance — it only addresses microbial (germ) contamination.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

The most common mistake: cooling it the wrong way

Adding ice or refrigerating water right after boiling can undo the benefit — it cuts short the time the water spends hot enough to finish killing germs, and ice or a cold container can itself introduce contamination. Let boiled water cool on its own at room temperature instead.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Agencies don't all use the same altitude cutoff

Public-health agencies vary slightly on the altitude that calls for the longer 3-minute boil — the EPA's threshold is 5,000 feet (about 1,000 m), while some other agencies use 6,500 feet. PlainWater follows the EPA's lower, more cautious threshold: when in doubt, the extra 2 minutes costs little and adds a safety margin.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Chemical treatment guide

Iodine, chlorine, bleach — what each removes vs. leaves behind.

What chemical disinfection does and doesn't do

Chlorine (bleach) and iodine kill or inactivate most bacteria and viruses effectively. They're less effective against Cryptosporidium, a parasite with a tough outer shell — boiling or filtration is more reliable against it. Chemical disinfection also doesn't remove chemical contaminants, heavy metals, or turbidity.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Cloudy water needs extra steps first

Chemical disinfectants work less effectively in cloudy or colored water. Let cloudy water settle, then filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter before disinfecting — and use double the normal dose of disinfectant.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Iodine isn't recommended for everyone

Pregnant people, people with thyroid conditions, and people with known iodine sensitivity should avoid iodine-based disinfection and use chlorine/bleach or boiling instead.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Bleach/chlorine dosing calculator

Exact drops per liter/gallon by concentration and contact time.

Only use plain, unscented household bleach

Use regular unscented liquid chlorine bleach intended for disinfection, containing 5%–9% sodium hypochlorite as the only active ingredient. Never use scented bleach, splash-less bleach, or bleach with added cleaners.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Check the bottle's age, not just its label

Bleach weakens as it sits, especially in heat or sunlight. The EPA specifies using bleach stored at room temperature for less than one year. An old, faded, or long-stored bottle may not disinfect as effectively even if you dose it exactly per the table below.

  • Use bleach stored at room temperature for less than 1 year
Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Dosing table

Check your bottle's label for the exact percentage, then dose per volume of water: 1 quart/liter — 2 drops (either 6% or 8.25% bleach). 1 gallon — 8 drops of 6% bleach, or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach. 2 gallons — 16 drops (about ¼ tsp) of 6%, or 12 drops (about ⅛ tsp) of 8.25%. 4 gallons — about ⅓ tsp of 6%, or ¼ tsp of 8.25%. 8 gallons — about ⅔ tsp of 6%, or ½ tsp of 8.25%.

  • 1 gallon: 8 drops (6% bleach) or 6 drops (8.25% bleach)
  • Double the dose if water is cloudy, colored, or very cold
Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Contact time

Stir well and let the water stand for 30 minutes before drinking. Properly treated water should have a slight chlorine odor — if it doesn't, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.

  • Wait 30 minutes minimum before drinking
Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Purification tablet dosing guide

Chlorine dioxide and iodine tablets, brand-agnostic.

Always follow the product's own label

Commercial disinfection tablets vary in strength and contact-time requirements by product and manufacturer — the number of tablets per liter/quart and the required wait time are printed on the package and must be followed exactly, since they differ between products.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Liquid iodine as a reference point

As a general reference (not a substitute for a tablet's own instructions): the EPA's liquid-iodine guidance is 5 drops of 2% tincture of iodine per quart/liter of clear water, or 10 drops if the water is cloudy, followed by a stand time before drinking.

  • Liquid iodine reference: 5 drops per quart/liter (2% tincture), double if cloudy
Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Same limitations as other chemical disinfection

Tablets are less reliable against Cryptosporidium than boiling or filtration, and shouldn't be relied on for chemical contamination. Iodine tablets carry the same cautions as liquid iodine for pregnant people and those with thyroid conditions.

Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

UV/solar disinfection (SODIS) step-by-step

With cloud-cover and exposure-time adjustments.

What SODIS is

Solar water disinfection (SODIS) uses sunlight's UV-A radiation plus heat to inactivate germs in water. It's a recognized low-cost household water treatment method when boiling or chemical disinfection aren't available, but it's slower and more weather-dependent than either.

Source: CAWST — Household Water Treatment and Safe Storage

How to do it

Use a clean, clear (not tinted) plastic bottle — PET beverage bottles are the standard choice. If the water is cloudy, filter or let it settle first; SODIS doesn't work well in turbid water. Fill the bottle, lay it on its side in full direct sunlight, and leave it for the required exposure time before drinking.

Source: CAWST — Household Water Treatment and Safe Storage

Exposure time depends on the weather

In full, direct sunlight, expose the bottle for at least 6 hours. If the sky is cloudy (roughly 50% cloud cover or more), it's not reliable in a single day — leave the bottle out for 2 consecutive days instead. SODIS generally isn't reliable during continuous heavy rain or full overcast; treat it as a fallback method for clear-to-partly-cloudy conditions, not an all-weather solution.

  • Sunny: at least 6 hours
  • Cloudy: 2 consecutive days
Source: CDC — Water Disinfection for Travelers (Yellow Book)
