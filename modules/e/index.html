Emergency water-sourcing guide

Rainwater, water-heater tank, ice, digging a well, and which sources are safest.

Baseline: one gallon per person per day

Ready.gov recommends storing at least one gallon of water per person per day, for drinking and sanitation combined, for at least several days. A normally active person needs about three-quarters of a gallon of that from water and other beverages; the rest covers sanitation and food prep.

  • Baseline: 1 gallon per person per day
Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Water

Finding water when your stored supply runs out

Emergency sources include a home's water heater tank, ice, and collected rainwater; in a longer emergency, a hand-dug or driven well is a last-resort option. None of these are automatically safe to drink as found.

Source: CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply

Treat before you drink, every time

Any water sourced this way — tank, ice, rainwater, a found well — should be treated using one of the purification methods in this app before drinking, regardless of how it looks.

Source: CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply

Storage best practices

How long purified/stored water stays safe, by method.

How long self-stored water stays good

If you fill your own containers with treated tap water, replace the water every 6 months. For store-bought bottled water, follow the printed expiration date.

  • Rotate self-filled containers every 6 months
Source: CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply

Where to keep it

Store containers somewhere cool (roughly 50–70°F), out of direct sunlight, and away from anything toxic like gasoline or pesticides. Label each container "drinking water" with the date it was filled.

Source: CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply

Storage container hygiene & recontamination prevention

Keeping stored water safe over time.

Choosing a container

A good storage container has a narrow neck or opening (so water pours out without hands or objects going in) and holds a standard, manageable volume with a comfortable handle. Never reuse a container that previously held toxic chemicals — bleach, pesticides, or petroleum products.

Source: CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply

Washing and sanitizing before you fill it

Wash the container with soap and water and rinse completely. Then sanitize it with a solution of 1 teaspoon of unscented liquid household chlorine bleach in 1 quart (4 cups) of water.

  • Sanitizing ratio: 1 tsp unscented bleach per quart of water
Source: CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply

Keeping it clean once it's in use

A narrow-neck design that lets you pour rather than dip is itself a recontamination safeguard — avoid putting hands, cups, or other objects directly into open storage water.

Source: CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply

Water-needs calculator

Per person/day for drinking, cooking, hygiene — normal vs. emergency rationing.

Baseline figure

Plan on one gallon of water per person per day, covering both drinking and sanitation.

  • 1 gallon / person / day
Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Water

Who needs more than the baseline

Individual needs vary with age, health, activity, diet, and climate. Children, nursing mothers, and people who are sick typically need more; in hot temperatures, water needs can double.

Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) — Water

Planning for longer emergencies

The 1-gallon figure is the daily baseline; for planning purposes beyond an initial few days, build toward a larger reserve rather than assuming a short outage.

Source: CDC — How to Create an Emergency Water Supply

Well/borehole safety basics

Contamination warning signs, seasonal risk (post-flood), safe distance from septic systems.

Private wells are your responsibility

Public drinking-water regulations don't apply to privately owned wells — testing and safety are on the well owner.

Source: CDC — Well Water Safety

Test at least once a year

Test annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH — and test again any time there's flooding, land disturbance, a new waste-disposal site nearby, or a change in taste, color, or smell.

  • Test at least once a year; more often after flooding or a change in taste/color/smell
Source: CDC — Well Water Safety

Shallower wells carry more risk

Dug wells, bored wells, and other wells less than 50 feet deep are more likely to be contaminated — even when there's no visible sign of damage.

  • Wells under 50 feet deep: higher contamination risk
Source: CDC — Well Water Safety

After a flood

A flooded well needs to be tested and disinfected before you trust it again. Until then, drink bottled water or another treated source.

Source: CDC — How to Disinfect Wells After an Emergency

Distance from a septic system

There's no single universal "safe distance." Required setbacks between a well and a septic system vary widely by local soil type, groundwater flow, and regulation — research has found appropriate distances ranging from well under 15 meters to over 300 meters depending on conditions. Contact your local health or environmental department for the actual requirement where you live.

Source: EPA — Septic Systems and Drinking Water

Rainwater harvesting safety guide

First-flush diversion, roof-material safety, storage-tank hygiene.

What washes off your roof

Roof runoff picks up metals or hydrocarbons from roofing materials, dust, and bird droppings. Collection should be limited to above-ground, waterproof roofing surfaces.

Sources: CDC — Collecting Rainwater and Your Health: An Overview; EPA — Rainwater Harvesting Policies

First-flush diversion

Divert the first flow of rainwater in a storm — it carries the most dirt, germs, and chemicals — away from your collection container. Note that first-flush diverters reduce dirt and chemical contamination more effectively than they reduce microbial (germ) counts, so diversion alone isn't a substitute for treatment.

Source: EPA — Rainwater Harvesting Policies

Keep the whole system clean

Regularly maintaining your rainwater collection system — gutters, screens, and storage container — helps keep germs and chemicals out of the water you collect.

Source: CDC — Collecting Rainwater and Your Health: An Overview

Treat it before drinking

Collected rainwater should be treated before drinking, the same as any other untreated source — collection alone doesn't make it safe.

Source: CDC — Collecting Rainwater and Your Health: An Overview
