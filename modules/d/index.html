Waterborne illness symptom guide

Cholera, giardia, dysentery, typhoid, traveler's diarrhea — what to watch for, when it's an emergency.

Cholera

Severe watery diarrhea, often with vomiting, that can cause life-threatening dehydration within hours. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) is the most important treatment, and many people can be fully rehydrated with ORS alone.

Source: CDC — Treating Cholera

Traveler's diarrhea

Symptoms range from mild cramps and loose stool to bloody diarrhea, fever, abdominal pain, and vomiting. Course depends on cause: bacterial diarrhea usually lasts 3–7 days, viral 2–3 days, and protozoal (e.g. giardia) can persist for weeks to months without treatment.

Source: CDC — Travelers' Diarrhea

When traveler's diarrhea needs a doctor

Seek medical attention if symptoms persist 24–36 hours, are particularly severe, or include a high fever, dehydration, or bloody diarrhea. If severe diarrhea develops soon after returning from a trip, see a doctor.

Source: CDC — Travelers' Diarrhea

Giardia, dysentery, typhoid — a shared pattern

These present differently in detail but share a common thread: diarrheal illness from contaminated water or food that risks dehydration the longer it goes untreated. Rehydration comes first; a persistent, worsening, or bloody presentation is the signal to get a diagnosis and treatment, not just to wait it out.

Sources: CDC — Travelers' Diarrhea; CDC — Treating Cholera

Giardia (giardiasis)

Diarrhea, gas, bloating, greasy or foul-smelling stools, stomach cramps, nausea, and sometimes weight loss. Symptoms usually start 1–2 weeks after exposure and can last 2–6 weeks. About half of infections cause no symptoms at all, but an infected person can still pass it on to others.

Source: CDC — Symptoms of Giardia Infection

Shigella (bacterial dysentery)

Diarrhea — often bloody — fever, and stomach cramps, usually starting just 1–2 days after exposure. Most cases resolve in about 5–7 days. Blood or mucus in the stool is a hallmark of dysentery and is a reason to get medical care rather than wait it out.

Source: CDC — Signs and Symptoms of Shigella Infection

Typhoid fever

A fever that climbs day by day — often reaching 103–104°F (39–40°C) by the third or fourth day — plus weakness, stomach pain, headache, diarrhea or constipation, cough, and loss of appetite; some people develop flat, rose-colored spots on the skin. Left untreated, the fever can persist for weeks and lead to serious complications. Typhoid needs medical treatment (usually antibiotics) — it does not reliably resolve with rehydration alone the way milder diarrheal illness can, so get medical help promptly.

Source: CDC — Symptoms of Typhoid Fever and Paratyphoid Fever

Preventing spread to others in the household

Diarrheal illness often spreads hand-to-mouth, not just through the water itself. Wash hands thoroughly with soap for at least 20 seconds — including the backs of hands, between fingers, and under nails — after using the bathroom, after changing a diaper, and before preparing or eating food. Handwashing alone cuts diarrheal illness substantially: community handwashing education reduces it by roughly 23–40% overall, and by about 58% in people with weakened immune systems.

Source: CDC — About Handwashing

Red-flag emergency triage

A clear line between home care and "seek help now."

Home care is appropriate when...

Symptoms are mild (loose stool, mild cramps), the person is drinking and urinating normally, and there's no blood, high fever, or severe pain. Keep up fluids with ORS and monitor.

Source: CDC — Travelers' Diarrhea

Seek care now if...

Diarrhea is bloody, a high fever is present, symptoms are severe, or diarrhea persists more than 24–36 hours without improving. In infants 2 months or younger, any diarrhea or vomiting warrants an immediate call to a clinician.

Sources: CDC — Travelers' Diarrhea; CDC — Caregivers of Infants and Young Children

Do not wait once dehydration signs appear

Dehydration can happen quickly, especially in young children. If you see warning signs — very little or no urination, dark urine, dizziness, dry mouth, or (in infants) a sunken soft spot or no tears when crying — start rehydration immediately and don't wait to see if it resolves on its own.

Source: CDC — When to Get Medical Help for Fluid Loss (Dehydration)

Home oral rehydration solution (ORS) calculator

Precise salt/sugar/water ratios; critical for diarrheal illness, especially in children.

The standard home recipe

1 liter (about 1 quart) of the cleanest water available, 6 level teaspoons of sugar, and 1/2 level teaspoon of salt. Use standard measuring spoons, not silverware — the salt amount matters and shouldn't be estimated by eye.

  • 1 L water + 6 level tsp sugar + 1/2 level tsp salt
Source: CDC — Prepare Oral Rehydration Solution from One Liter of Water

If tap water quality is in question

Boil the water for a few minutes and let it cool before mixing in the sugar and salt.

Source: CDC — Prepare Oral Rehydration Solution from One Liter of Water

Too much salt is dangerous

Never guess at the salt measurement or add extra "to be safe" — over-concentrated salt solution can itself cause harm (salt poisoning), especially in children. Measure exactly.

Source: CDC — Prepare Oral Rehydration Solution from One Liter of Water

Storage

Use homemade ORS within 24 hours if kept at room temperature, or within 48 hours if refrigerated. Discard and mix a fresh batch after that.

Source: CDC — Prepare Oral Rehydration Solution from One Liter of Water

Prepackaged ORS

Where available, a prepackaged oral rehydration salts sachet mixed with water (per its label) is at least as good as the homemade recipe and removes any measurement risk — it's the WHO/UNICEF-standard formulation.

Source: WHO — Oral Rehydration Salts: Production of the New ORS

Dehydration severity self-assessment (adult)

Simple staged self-check.

Early signs

Increased thirst, dry mouth or throat, and darker-than-usual urine. This is the point to start drinking ORS or extra fluids, not wait.

Source: CDC — When to Get Medical Help for Fluid Loss (Dehydration)

Worsening signs

Little or no urination, very dark urine, dizziness or lightheadedness, and a persistently dry mouth despite drinking.

Source: CDC — When to Get Medical Help for Fluid Loss (Dehydration)

Severe — this is an emergency

Confusion, fainting or near-fainting, inability to keep fluids down, or no urination for an extended period. Seek medical care immediately; severe dehydration often requires IV fluids.

Source: CDC — When to Get Medical Help for Fluid Loss (Dehydration)

Children's dehydration signs & care guide

Signs and care distinct from adult presentation.

Signs of dehydration in a child

Fewer than 6 wet diapers a day (or noticeably less frequent urination in an older child), sleepiness or low energy, unusual fussiness, dry mouth/tongue/lips, sunken eyes, few or no tears when crying, and cool or discolored hands or feet.

Source: CDC — When to Get Medical Help for Fluid Loss (Dehydration)

Sunken soft spot (infants)

A visibly sunken soft spot on top of an infant's head is a sign of moderate-to-severe dehydration and needs prompt medical attention.

Source: CDC — When to Get Medical Help for Fluid Loss (Dehydration)

What to do

Give extra fluids as soon as diarrhea or vomiting starts — ORS or an electrolyte solution like Pedialyte — rather than waiting for warning signs to appear. Don't wait once you see them.

Source: CDC — When to Get Medical Help for Fluid Loss (Dehydration)

Infant-specific hydration and formula-water guidance

Distinct from general adult guidance.

Call immediately if...

Any diarrhea or vomiting in an infant 2 months or younger warrants an immediate call to a clinician — don't wait to see if it resolves.

Source: CDC — Caregivers of Infants and Young Children

Watch for

A sunken soft spot, the baby seeming much less active or more irritable than normal, fewer tears when crying (or none), and less urine than usual.

Source: CDC — Caregivers of Infants and Young Children

Formula-mixing water

When water safety is uncertain, use the same treat-or-boil standard for formula-mixing water as for drinking water — infants are among the most vulnerable to both dehydration and contaminated water, so don't relax the standard for convenience.

Source: CDC — Caregivers of Infants and Young Children

Elderly-specific hydration guidance

Higher-risk dehydration considerations.

Why older adults are at higher risk

People aged 65 and older are more prone to heat-related illness and dehydration — reduced sweating, blunted thirst perception, and slower cardiovascular adjustment mean the body compensates less effectively than in younger adults.

Source: CDC — Heat and Older Adults (Aged 65+)

Compounding risk factors

Heart disease, prescription medications, alcohol use, obesity, and existing dehydration all further raise risk in older adults — worth extra caution during illness, heat, or travel.

Source: CDC — Heat and Older Adults (Aged 65+)

Act early, not late

Seek medical care immediately for symptoms like muscle cramps, headache, nausea, or vomiting in an older adult during a heat event or illness — don't wait for severe signs given the reduced physiological buffer.

Source: CDC — Heat and Older Adults (Aged 65+)

Immunocompromised-specific precautions

Stricter treatment thresholds.

Who this applies to

People with diabetes, liver or kidney disease, HIV, an autoimmune disorder, or those receiving chemotherapy, radiation, or transplant medications have a weakened ability to fight foodborne and waterborne illness.

Source: CDC — Safer Food Choices for People With Weakened Immune Systems

Stricter water precautions

In areas with uncertain water safety, avoid drinking or putting tap water in your mouth unless you're reasonably certain it's safe — brushing teeth included. This applies even when a lower-risk person might reasonably take the chance.

Source: CDC — Travelers with Weakened Immune Systems

When in doubt, treat every time

For an immunocompromised person, "probably fine" isn't a good enough bar — treat or boil water whenever there's any uncertainty, and seek medical care promptly (not just "if it gets bad") for any diarrheal illness.

Sources: CDC — Safer Food Choices for People With Weakened Immune Systems; CDC — Travelers with Weakened Immune Systems

Heat-stress/electrolyte guide

For laborers, athletes, and hikers — non-illness hydration.

Baseline water intake

For moderate activity in heat lasting under 2 hours, about 1 cup (8 oz) of water every 15–20 minutes is a reasonable baseline — drink before you feel thirsty rather than waiting for thirst.

Source: CDC / NIOSH — Workplace Recommendations — Heat Stress

When plain water isn't enough

During prolonged sweating over several hours, switch some intake to a sports drink with balanced electrolytes — plain water alone can under-replace sodium lost through heavy, sustained sweating.

Source: CDC / NIOSH — Workplace Recommendations — Heat Stress

Don't overdo it either

Fluid intake generally shouldn't exceed about 6 cups per hour. Regular meals and salty snacks are usually enough to replace electrolytes for most people; avoid alcohol and high-caffeine or high-sugar drinks, which work against hydration.

Source: CDC / NIOSH — Workplace Recommendations — Heat Stress

Take the breaks

Scheduled rest in shade or cooling is part of hydration strategy, not separate from it — cooling down reduces how much fluid and electrolyte loss you need to replace in the first place.

Source: CDC / NIOSH — Workplace Recommendations — Heat Stress

Everyday hydration — how much water you actually need

Baseline daily fluid needs for a healthy person, why "8 glasses a day" oversimplifies it, and the real risk of drinking too much.

The actual reference numbers

The U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine set adequate total-water intake (from all beverages and food combined) at about 3.7 liters/day for men and 2.7 liters/day for women, for healthy, sedentary adults in a temperate climate. About 80% of that total typically comes from drinking water and other beverages (including caffeinated ones), the other 20% from food.

  • Adequate total water intake: ~3.7 L/day (men), ~2.7 L/day (women) — from all beverages and food combined
Source: National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine — Report Sets Dietary Intake Levels for Water, Salt, and Potassium

"Drink 8 glasses a day" is a rough rule of thumb, not a hard target

It's a simple way to think about roughly half of a typical adult's total daily fluid needs, but the real requirement varies a lot by body size, activity, climate, and diet — and much of your water already comes from food. Don't treat it as a number to hit exactly; treat your body's own signals as the better guide.

Source: National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine — Report Sets Dietary Intake Levels for Water, Salt, and Potassium

A practical everyday check: urine color

Pale yellow urine generally means you're drinking enough; noticeably dark yellow urine is a sign to drink more. It's not a precise measurement, but it's a genuinely useful everyday indicator that doesn't require any equipment.

Source: NIDDK (NIH) — Prevention of Bladder Control Problems & Bladder Health

What increases your daily need

Heat, altitude, pregnancy, breastfeeding, fever or illness, and physical activity all raise fluid needs above the baseline. See this module's heat-stress guide, and the elderly- and infant-specific hydration guides, for details on those situations specifically.

Source: National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine — Report Sets Dietary Intake Levels for Water, Salt, and Potassium

Yes, you can drink too much water

Drinking far more water than your body can process dilutes the sodium in your blood (hyponatremia), which can cause nausea, headache, confusion, muscle weakness or cramps, and — in severe, fast-onset cases — seizures or worse. It's uncommon in ordinary daily life, but it's a real risk during endurance exercise (drinking beyond thirst over many hours) and is why sports drinks with electrolytes, not just plain water, are recommended for long endurance events.

Source: MedlinePlus (NIH/NLM) — Hyponatremia (low blood sodium)

Infants are a special case — don't give plain water to babies under 6 months

An infant's kidneys aren't developed enough to handle excess plain water, which can dilute their blood sodium dangerously — this has caused real, documented seizure cases from infants given plain water or over-diluted formula. Breast milk or properly-mixed formula meets an infant's fluid needs for the first 6 months; see this module's infant-specific hydration guide.

Source: CDC (MMWR) — Hyponatremic Seizures Among Infants Fed with Commercial Bottled Drinking Water — Wisconsin, 1993

Some medical conditions mean less water, not more

This module's baseline guidance assumes healthy kidneys, heart, and liver. People with kidney failure (especially on dialysis), certain heart failure diagnoses, or advanced liver disease are often told by their care team to limit fluids — because their body can't clear excess fluid the way a healthy body can, and too much can cause dangerous swelling, blood-pressure changes, or strain on the heart and lungs. There's no universal safe number for these conditions; it has to be set individually with a clinician or dietitian. If you have one of these diagnoses, follow your care team's specific fluid target, not this module's general guidance.

Source: NIH / NIDDK — Eating & Nutrition for Hemodialysis

Unexplained, persistent thirst can be a medical sign, not just under-hydration

Ongoing excessive thirst that doesn't go away no matter how much you drink — especially alongside frequent urination, fatigue, or blurred vision — can be a sign of uncontrolled diabetes, or, less commonly, a distinct water-balance condition called diabetes insipidus. This is different from the everyday thirst covered above. If thirst is persistent and unexplained, see a doctor rather than assuming you just need to drink more.

Source: NIH / MedlinePlus — Thirst - excessive
