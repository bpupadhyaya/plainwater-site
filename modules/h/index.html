Multi-language offline content

The highest-need populations are often non-English speakers.

Every core guide, in your language, no connection required

PlainWater's safety content ships bundled on your device in multiple languages, not fetched from a server. This is a deliberate design choice: the people who most need this information — including the roughly 2 billion people worldwide without reliable safe drinking water — are disproportionately likely to speak a language other than English and to have no reliable signal.

Unit conversion tool

Metric/imperial, liters/gallons.

Quick reference: volume

1 US gallon = 3.785 liters. 1 liter = 0.264 US gallons. 1 US quart = 0.946 liters. These are the standard defined conversion factors used throughout PlainWater's calculators (water-needs, dosing) so you can work in whichever unit you have on hand — a marked bottle, a bucket, a jerry can.

  • 1 US gallon = 3.785 L
  • 1 L = 0.264 US gallons

Quick reference: temperature

°F to °C: subtract 32, then multiply by 5/9. °C to °F: multiply by 9/5, then add 32. Relevant for altitude-adjusted boiling guidance and cold-weather storage notes elsewhere in the app.

Myth-busting FAQ

Bottled vs. tap, boiling myths, etc.

Myth: "Boiling water removes everything harmful."

False, and an important one to get right. Boiling kills disease-causing organisms — bacteria, viruses, and parasites — extremely effectively. It does nothing for chemical contaminants: heavy metals, salts, fuel, pesticides, and most other chemicals are not destroyed by boiling, and in fact become slightly more concentrated as water evaporates. If you suspect chemical contamination (a fuel smell, a known spill, an industrial area), boiling will not make that water safe.

  • Boiling: kills germs. Does not remove chemicals, metals, or salts.
Source: EPA — Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water

Myth: "Bottled water is always safer than tap water."

Not necessarily. In the US, tap water is regulated by the EPA and bottled water is regulated by the FDA, and both are held to safety standards — bottled water isn't automatically held to a stricter standard, and in some cases (like lead) the standards actually differ rather than one simply being tougher across the board. Where tap water fails to meet standards (or during a boil-water advisory), bottled or properly treated water is the safer choice — but "bottled" alone isn't a guarantee, and it doesn't help in an emergency where none is available.

Source: CDC — Drinking Water Standards and Regulations: An Overview

Myth: "If it looks and tastes fine, it's safe."

The most dangerous myth of all. Many serious contaminants — including disease-causing germs and chemicals like arsenic — typically have no color, taste, or odor. See the Visual & Sensory Assessment module for the full explanation: appearance is a useful signal, never a safety verdict.

Source: CDC — Well Water Safety

Myth: "My neighbor's well water is fine, so mine must be too."

Not necessarily, even next door. Each private well is its own separate system, drawing from its own depth and geology, with its own construction, age, and maintenance history — one neighbor's clean result says nothing definitive about another well a few hundred feet away. This is exactly why the CDC recommends every well owner test their own water independently, on their own schedule, rather than relying on a neighbor's result as a stand-in.

Source: CDC — Well Water Safety

"Community water fluoridation is unsafe or unproven"

Not according to the evidence. The CDC names water fluoridation one of the ten great U.S. public-health achievements of the 20th century: adding fluoride to a community's water supply to a target of about 0.7 mg/L reduces tooth decay by roughly 25% in children and adults, and it's endorsed by the American Dental Association, American Academy of Pediatrics, and World Health Organization, based on more than 75 years of research. This is separate from testing your own water's fluoride level (see Module B) — this entry is about the public-health practice itself, which is well-studied and considered safe at recommended levels.

Source: CDC — Community Water Fluoridation Facts

Glossary of water-quality terms

Plain-language definitions.

Turbidity

A measure of how cloudy water is, caused by suspended particles. Measured in NTU (nephelometric turbidity units). High turbidity can shield germs from disinfection, even when the germs themselves aren't visible.

MCL (Maximum Contaminant Level)

The legal, health-based limit the EPA sets for a contaminant in US public drinking water — enforceable, tied to health effects.

SMCL (Secondary Maximum Contaminant Level)

A non-enforceable EPA guideline for taste, odor, color, or staining — not a health limit. Exceeding an SMCL is a nuisance issue, not automatically a safety issue.

Potable

Safe to drink.

Coliform bacteria

A broad group of bacteria used as an indicator: their presence in water suggests the water may be contaminated with germs from feces, even if the coliform bacteria themselves aren't the dangerous ones.

TDS (Total Dissolved Solids)

The combined amount of minerals, salts, and metals dissolved in water, measured in mg/L. High TDS affects taste and can indicate hardness, but isn't by itself a health measure.

SODIS (Solar Water Disinfection)

A low-cost purification method: filling a clear plastic bottle with water and leaving it in full sun for several hours, using UV light and heat to inactivate germs. Recognized by the WHO and widely used in low-resource settings and disaster response. See the Purification Methods module for the full how-to.

Source: CAWST — Disinfection Method: SODIS

CCR (Consumer Confidence Report)

The annual water-quality report your US public water utility is required to send you, also called a "drinking water quality report." It lists your water's source, what was tested for, what was found, and any violations. See the Region & Advisory-Specific Guidance module for how to read one.

Source: EPA — CCR Information for Consumers

Action level

For contaminants like lead, where the EPA can't set a simple safe-concentration limit because the contamination usually comes from household plumbing rather than the water source, it instead sets an action level — a trigger point that requires the utility to take corrosion-control action, not a straightforward health-based limit the way an MCL is.

Source: EPA — Lead and Copper Rule
